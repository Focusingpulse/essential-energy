Do EMF protection pendants work?

Short answer: no, not in the way they are usually sold. I'll show you what's actually been tested, what regulators have found, and then tell you honestly why I wear one anyway.

The direct answer: independent testing has repeatedly found that EMF pendants do not measurably reduce the electromagnetic field around the wearer. Small stones and plates cannot block radio waves arriving from every direction. If a seller tells you a pendant shields you, that claim has not held up when it's been tested.

What happens when these are actually measured

An EMF pendant is easy to test. You put a meter on the wearer and see whether the reading changes. When testing was done on pendant and sticker products with standard RF meters, the meters did not move. No measurable drop.

There are physical reasons for that. Meaningfully blocking radiofrequency radiation requires a conductive barrier that surrounds the source or the person, which is how a Faraday cage works. A small pendant at your chest cannot intercept waves arriving from a cell tower, a Wi-Fi router, and a phone in your hand at the same time. The geometry doesn't allow it.

One exception worth knowing: shungite genuinely does conduct electricity and can attenuate certain frequencies — but only in bulk, as a continuous barrier. Building materials with 50–60% shungite can absorb meaningfully. A polished stone on a cord covers a few square centimeters of a body being exposed from every direction. The material property is real; it just doesn't transfer to pendant scale.

The FTC has acted on these claims

This isn't a fringe concern. In 2002 and 2003 the Federal Trade Commission brought cases against sellers of cell-phone radiation patches making claims of blocking "up to 97% or 99%" of electromagnetic energy. Independent tests — including by the Good Housekeeping Institute — found the products did not reduce exposure at all.

The resulting orders barred those companies from making radiation-blocking claims without competent and reliable scientific evidence.

What about EMF sensitivity?

Many people who buy these products are experiencing real symptoms and attribute them to electromagnetic fields. The symptoms are real. The attribution is what isn't supported.

The World Health Organization's position is that while these symptoms are genuine and can be disabling, there's no scientific basis connecting them to EMF exposure. Controlled double-blind studies have found that people reporting electromagnetic sensitivity cannot reliably tell whether a source is on or off — symptoms are reported at similar rates on sham exposure.

The WHO suggests the symptoms may instead relate to other environmental factors: lighting flicker, screen glare, air quality, workplace stress, or anxiety about the technology itself.

This does not mean the symptoms are imagined. It means the pendant is unlikely to be addressing what's causing them, and a doctor is a better first stop than a product page.

Some energy pendants are actually hazardous

Separately from whether they work: the Washington State Department of Health tested products sold as "energy pendants" and found unacceptable levels of radioactive material — thorium and uranium — in several of them. Some exceeded levels that would require a radioactive materials license. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission has also noted that some products marketed with negative ion, scalar, or quantum claims contain radioactive material.

That warning applies to mineral-based products specifically. Stainless steel plates, like the ones I wear, are not in that category. If you're shopping in this market, it's worth knowing which material you're looking at.

What actually reduces your exposure

The methods that work are free and boring, and they follow basic physics:

  • Increase distance. RF energy falls off rapidly with distance. A phone on a table instead of in your pocket is a real change.
  • Use speakerphone or wired headphones. Moving the phone away from your head is the single biggest reduction available to most people.
  • Text instead of calling when you can.
  • Avoid calls on a weak signal. A phone struggling to reach a tower increases its output.
  • Spend less time on calls.

No pendant substitutes for these. The National Cancer Institute's guidance on reducing RF exposure describes hands-free use and distance — not accessories.
